User guide

How to run an ASME PTC-10 Type 2 performance-test validation, start to finish. New users can follow the steps in order.

What this tool does

It validates a shop performance test of a centrifugal compressor run on a substitute gas (an ASME PTC-10 Type 2 test), against the contract guarantee per API 617. It sizes the required test gas and speed, checks that the test stays inside the code's allowable similitude limits (volume ratio, flow coefficient, Machine Mach and Reynolds numbers), compares corrected/as-measured performance to the guarantee, carries the API 617 + ADNOC AGES mechanical-running-test checklist, validates the vendor's test procedure and test report against the ASME PTC-10 Type 2 tutorial by Colby (2005), and produces a printable A4 report. Every calculated value shows the formula with your numbers substituted — nothing is hidden.

Volume reduction & similitude

Why a Type 2 test runs slower than the machine ever will, and what the code limits are actually protecting.

The premise

A Type 2 test is built on similitude of volume reduction. In a multistage machine the volume reduction of stage 1 fixes the capacity seen by stage 2, which fixes stage 3, and so on to the discharge. Once the selection is frozen, the design volume reduction of the rotor is frozen with it. For the shop test to say anything about field performance, the test volume reduction must equal the design volume reduction — that single requirement generates every other rule on these pages.

Volume reduction of a stage = V₂ / V₁ — the ratio of discharge to inlet volume. It depends on the polytropic head, the efficiency, the gas density and the gas properties.

Governing relations Colby Eq. 1–5

(1) Head = (1545 / MW) × T₁ × Z₁ × [n/(n−1)] × [ (P₂/P₁)^((n−1)/n) − 1 ]
(2) n/(n−1) = ηp × [ k/(k−1) ]   — polytropic volume exponent from efficiency and k
(3) Head = μ × U² / g   — head is the head coefficient times tip speed squared
(4) μ = Head × g / ΣU²   — overall μ for a multistage rotor
(5) Mm = U / A₀ = U / √(k g R T Z)   — machine Mach number, first-stage tip speed over inlet sonic velocity
Read Eq. (1) as an identity that must balance. Hold everything constant but one variable and the pressure ratio — and therefore the volume reduction — has to move to keep it balanced. That is the whole of Table 1 below.

Table 1 — effect on volume reduction Colby (2005), Table 1

VariableChangeEffect on volume reduction
Inlet pressure has no effect on volume reduction. The discharge pressure moves proportionately and the ratio is unchanged. Pressure reaches the volume reduction only through its effect on the gas properties k and Z. That is why suction pressure is free to be chosen for other reasons — Reynolds number, heat loss and available shop power (see Suction pressure).

Figure 1 — three-stage compressor, design vs. high mole weight

Figure 1. Stage curves of head versus inlet capacity. The solid marker on each stage is the design capacity; the dashed marker is where the stage actually operates when the gas mole weight is above specified. Stage 1 is barely displaced, but its extra volume reduction pushes stage 2 to a lower capacity, where it makes more head and reduces volume further — the error compounds stage by stage. A test on a heavier gas therefore has to have its head, and so its speed, reduced to bring the overall volume reduction back to design.

Figure 2 — stage map at three machine Mach numbers

Figure 2. Normalised head coefficient and efficiency against normalised inlet flow coefficient. Machine Mach number changes both the level and the width of the map: the higher the Mach number the narrower the operating range and the steeper the head curve. Because μ sets the head and the head sets the volume reduction, a Mach number departure between test and design is a volume-reduction error — which is exactly why PTC-10 Fig. 3.3 tightens the allowable departure as design Mach number rises, and why the departure is checked at the first stage only, all later stages being assumed to depart by the same percentage.

Determining the test conditions

What the manufacturer controls, how the test speed falls out of volume-ratio similitude, and where the inherent errors are.

What the manufacturer can choose

Inlet temperatureSet by the cooling capacity of the test stand — typically about 100 °F (38 °C). Any difference from design is absorbed by the test speed.
Test gas mediumAlmost always higher in mole weight than the specified gas, so the required speed stays below MCS. Second criterion is the closest k value.
Inlet pressureNo effect on volume reduction. Chosen for mass flow, and hence power draw, Reynolds number and heat loss. Often near 10 % of the specified value.
Test speedThe dependent variable — set so the test volume ratio equals the design volume ratio.

Deriving the test speed Colby Eq. 6–10

(6) P Vn = constant
(7) P₁ V₁n = P₂ V₂n
(8) V₁/V₂ = (P₂/P₁)1/n
(9) (V₁/V₂)test = (V₁/V₂)specified   — the similitude condition
(10) Ntest = Nspecified × √( Headtest / Headspecified )
The sequence: efficiency is taken as equal test-to-design, so Eq. (2) gives the test polytropic exponent n from the test gas k. Eq. (8) with (9) then gives the test pressure ratio that reproduces the design volume ratio. Eq. (1) converts that to the required test head, and Eq. (10) converts head to speed, head being proportional to speed squared.

Inherent errors in a Type 2 test

  • The speed calculation assumes a straight-line polytropic path on the pressure–enthalpy diagram between the endpoints. The real path is slightly curved.
  • The Mach number departure is checked at the first stage only. It is not equal at every stage of a multistage machine.
  • The compressibility change from inlet to discharge is normally larger at the specified condition than at the low-pressure test condition.
  • Each of these perturbs the individual stage volume reductions away from design. Their combined effect only shows up in a stage-by-stage calculation — which is the next page.

Stage-by-stage volume-reduction review

The check the code does not ask for and that decides whether the test is representative. Ask the vendor for it by name.

Why the code limits are not enough

The paper's sample compressor sits inside every PTC-10 Table 3.2 limit at its code-derived test speed of 11,070 rpm — and still produces a test curve that does not lie on the specified curve. The overall departures pass; the stage-by-stage behaviour does not. The only way to see it is to have the manufacturer calculate the test condition stage by stage, convert to μ, η and φ, and plot the test curve on the predicted specified-gas curve.

What to request in the test agenda: (a) the predicted test curve plotted on the predicted specified-gas curve in dimensionless form (μ and η versus φ), and (b) the normalised flow coefficient at every stage, inlet flange to discharge flange, at the design inlet flow coefficient.

Choosing the test suction pressure

Volume reduction does not care about inlet pressure. Power draw, Reynolds number and heat loss to atmosphere care a great deal.

The three competing constraints

Shop driver powerInlet pressure sets inlet density, hence mass flow, hence power. The test must run inside the power limit of the available shop driver — an upper bound on pressure.
Reynolds numberTest Re is directly proportional to inlet pressure. A Type 2 test is nearly always at a lower Re than specified, which raises frictional losses and depresses both head and efficiency below what the field will see — a lower bound on pressure.
Heat loss to atmosphereConvection and radiation through the casing subtract from the measured enthalpy rise. Since the loss is roughly fixed while the mass throughput grows with pressure, the loss as a percentage of work input falls as pressure rises — another lower bound.
On the paper's sample compressor the two effects very nearly cancel: at 44 psia the Reynolds correction is about +0.5 % and the heat loss about 0.44 % of total heat input. That is not an accident — it is what a well-chosen suction pressure looks like.

Figure 8 — Reynolds number correction Colby (2005), Fig. 8

Figure 8. Head and efficiency correction factor against test Reynolds number for the sample compressor, with the corresponding test suction pressure and percentage of specified Reynolds number on the same abscissa. The correction is not linear — it grows exponentially as absolute Reynolds number falls. For this machine the allowable test range is 10 % to 138 % of the specified value; at the 10 % floor the correction would be about 1.2 %. The absolute floor set by the code is Rem = 90,000 on the test value. The suction-pressure and percent-of-specified traces share the abscissa but carry their own scales — 0…160 psia and 0…120 % respectively, mapped onto the plot height — exactly as the figure is published.
Most users do not permit the manufacturer to correct test data for Reynolds number. Whether or not correction is allowed, the manufacturer should pick a suction pressure that keeps the correction at its lowest practical level, and the user should know what that level is. Both verification programs on the Generic tabs exist for exactly this — one with correction applied, one without.

Figure 9 — convection and radiation losses Colby (2005), Fig. 9

Figure 9. Casing heat loss as a percentage of total heat input, against test suction pressure. Below roughly 30 psia the loss climbs steeply. Heat lost to atmosphere subtracts from the measured work input, so if it is not minimised, eliminated or accounted for, the heat-balance efficiency comes out falsely high. Where the loss cannot be brought down, insulate the casing for the performance test.

Test loop & recirculation

API 617 guarantees power, not efficiency. Power is mass × work input — so the leakages have to be measured, not assumed.

Why leakage is a power question

(11) GHP = Head × Mass / (33,000 × η)
(12) mass = C × π × D × S × √( (Ph − Pl) × ρh )   — seal leakage, orifice-like

At the specified operating point API 617 7th Edition requires the power to be guaranteed within 4 % of quoted; efficiency is not guaranteed. Power is the product of the total mass compressed and the work input, so any recirculation or leakage that is mispredicted lands directly on the power number. Wherever the loop permits it, leakages should be measured during the shop test and the measured values used when converting results to the specified condition.

Manufacturers do not all use the same leakage equation, but the physics is common. With the leakage measured, invert the equation to get a pseudo clearance — pseudo because it also absorbs any error in the constant C — and use that to convert the results.

The standard test procedure & surge

What a normal test agenda contains, when to ask for more, and the five ways of finding the minimum stable flow.

The standard agenda

  • Conducted at the design point speed only, in the normal case.
  • Five test points at equally spaced capacities from overload to surge, one of them at the design inlet flow coefficient.
  • The compressor is brought to thermal equilibrium before each point is recorded.
  • Surge points at two alternate speeds, to define the slope of the surge line. These are normally not thermally stable when recorded — head and flow coefficient are the objective there, not efficiency.
  • For a constant-speed machine the alternate points establish the surge line slope for off-design mole weight.
The three surge points also say where surge begins. Surge points close together in flow coefficient mean surge is initiated early — stage 1 or 2. A wide spread between them points to one of the later stages.

Figure 12 — the transition into surge

Figure 12. A unit throttled slowly toward surge with the data system recomputing performance every four seconds. The head coefficient stops rising and droops, then recovers and rises again until audible surge is finally reached. Subsynchronous vibration appeared at the bottom of the droop. The minimum stable flow was recorded at the initial peak head — some 10 % higher in capacity than the audible surge point. Determining surge by ear alone would have understated the machine's turndown requirement.

Five criteria for the minimum stable flow point

Audible surgeFlow reversal is plainly audible. Historically the only method — and often not the capacity at which the machine actually surges in the field.
Onset of subsynchronous vibrationAppears at the onset of surge provided there is enough energy input. The frequency identifies the source: 6–16 % of running speed suggests a stall in the stationary flow-path components; 70–90 % indicates the instability starts in the impellers.
Peak headIn many cases the head falls away before anything else is noticed — often the earliest and most defensible indicator.
Flow instabilityReadily evident on the flow measurement.
Pressure instabilityReadily evident on the pressure measurement.
Modern data acquisition and vibration monitoring make surge determination a measurement rather than a judgement. The test report should state which criterion was used — the choice can move the declared minimum stable flow by around 10 %.

When to buy an extra speed line

It is a risk-assessment question, and it starts with the process engineer: which operating conditions actually matter for the process to run. The paper's rule of thumb — compare the machine Mach number at the alternate condition with that at the specified point. If the variance is greater than the code would allow between test and design, an additional speed line may be warranted. Also review how the manufacturer extrapolates the design-point test data to alternate operating points; the method varies by manufacturer and a weak extrapolation is itself an argument for testing the second line.

Type 1 testing

Same principles, specified gas, and a tolerance the author recommends tightening well inside the code.

What changes, and what does not

A Type 1 test is run on the specified gas at or very near the specified conditions, with deviations limited by Tables 3.1 and 3.2 of the code. Every principle from the Type 2 discussion still applies. In practice the specified gas is rarely available and the specified inlet temperature is often out of reach of the test stand's cooling capacity, so a substitute is blended — typically from a local near-methane supply with carbon dioxide, propane or another gas added to hit the target mole weight.

The blend must carry a mixture k value very close to the specified gas. That is what keeps the machine Mach number right and makes the thermodynamic conversion of work input produce the same pressure ratio. If the test inlet temperature has to be higher than specified, the test mole weight must go up to offset it — while still holding the k value.

The R·T·Z product Colby's recommendation

Equations (1) and (5) share the product of the gas constant R = 1545/MW, the inlet temperature and the inlet compressibility. If a blend has the same k and the same inlet R·T·Z product, the performance will be the same as on the specified gas. The author recommends holding the inlet R·T·Z product and the inlet pressure within ± 2 % of specified — considerably tighter than PTC-10 Table 3.2 permits.
